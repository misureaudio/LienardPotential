**What checks out (I verified these by hand/symbolically and numerically):**

- The Hamiltonian decomposition (3)–(4): $\dot H = \tfrac{d}{dt}(v^2/2+V)= -f(x)v^2$ — correct, direct computation.
- The divergence argument (14): in both the Liénard plane and the physical plane, $\operatorname{div}=-f(x)$, so the flow is non-symplectic exactly when $f\not\equiv0$ — correct application of Liouville's theorem.
- The symbolic symplectic checks in §2.1: I recomputed the Störmer–Verlet map and the implicit-midpoint endpoint map for $V=x^2/2$ from scratch and got $\det M=1$ in both cases, exactly matching (7)–(8) as printed, including the reported non-symplecticity of the *midpoint* (as opposed to endpoint) map, $\det=4/(4+h^2)$.
- The stability-function table (§2.3): $R(z)=(1+z/2)/(1-z/2)$ for implicit midpoint, $R(-\infty)=-1$; the general fact that the whole Gauss-Legendre family has $R(-\infty)=(-1)^s$ (so *no* Gauss method is $L$-stable) is a true, well-known result, not just an artifact of the two examples shown.
- The drift-rate scaling in §4.1–4.2: I checked the exponents against their own tables — RK4's secular error indeed scales like $h^{p+1}=h^5$ and Störmer–Verlet's bounded envelope like $h^2$, matching backward-error-analysis theory.
- The $\mu=100$ period: I integrated the actual van der Pol equation independently (SciPy's Radau, tight tolerance, long transient to settle onto the cycle) and got $T=162.83707$, matching their quoted $162.8371$ almost to the last digit.

**What I could *not* reproduce:** their headline claim in §6.3 that implicit midpoint "escapes to $\sim10^{30}$" on the $\mu=100$ cycle, even at $h=0.01$. I implemented implicit midpoint properly (Newton iteration with the exact Jacobian, tight convergence tolerance) and ran it for 3–20 periods at $h=0.5,0.1,0.01$. The method does behave badly — it shows large spurious transients when crossing the fast jump (values up to ~100–150 against a true amplitude of ~2, consistent with the "$A$-stable but not $L$-stable → ringing" mechanism they describe) — but it does **not** blow up to $10^{29}$–$10^{32}$; it stays bounded and even partially recovers, over runs as long as 20 periods.

So the *mechanism* they invoke (Gauss methods aren't $L$-stable, so they can't kill the fast mode, so they mishandle the relaxation jump) is real and correctly explained — but the specific magnitude quoted for the "verified" blow-up looks off, possibly from a solver bug in their own script (e.g. a fixed-point iteration that fails to converge rather than a true Newton solve, which can produce spurious garbage that looks like blow-up). I'd treat that one number range with real suspicion and, if this essay is meant to be relied on, ask to see `geometric_integration_computation*.py` and re-run it rather than taking the reported figures on faith — the rest of the derivation doesn't depend on the exact magnitude anyway, since "large uncontrolled error" is already enough to make their qualitative point.
